\begin{abstract}
  Deep learning theory and practice increasingly emphasize the \emph{geometry} of the empirical risk near trained parameters, not only the risk value.
  A natural question is how that local geometry changes when the training set grows by one example: classical uniform convergence describes the population risk as a function, but leaves open how gradients and curvature near minimizers evolve with sample size.
  Prior work introduced landscape \emph{increment} measures for $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}-\mathcal{L}_k$ near a minimizer of $\mathcal{L}_k$, including an absolute one-point criterion and a full-space mean-squared criterion; the latter was shown to decay as $\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})$ under standard per-example bounds.
  Yet full-space mean-squared probing in $\mathbb{R}^N$ remains expensive and often wasteful when curvature concentrates in a small number of Hessian directions, so a gap remains for a criterion aligned with principal curvature while preserving the mean-squared rate.
  This paper studies the \emph{subspace} mean-squared criterion $\Delta_2^{(D)}$, which restricts the same squared increment to perturbations along the top-$D$ eigenvectors of the empirical Hessian at a local minimum.
  Using a local quadratic model, we prove an $\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})$ upper bound in which the curvature contribution scales with~$D$ rather than the ambient dimension~$N$, give a closed-form spectral expression under optional alignment of principal directions across successive empirical risks, and describe estimation via Hessian--vector products, randomized eigenpair solvers, and Monte Carlo in $\mathbb{R}^D$.
  Our empirical program is centered on nanoGPT-style decoder-only transformers, pairing a three-seed Tiny Shakespeare reference run with a stronger WikiText-2 validation run and reviewer-facing ablations on subspace choice, locality, and eigenspace drift.
  In the reference run, log--log fits give slopes of approximately $-1.08$ for $\Delta_1$, $-1.93$ for $\Delta_2$, and $-1.99$ for $\Delta_2^{(10)}$; in the WikiText-2 run, the corresponding slopes are $-0.97$, $-2.24$, and $-1.97$.
  The results make explicit how mean-squared landscape stability can be stated and measured in an intrinsic dimension matched to dominant curvature rather than to the full parameter count.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Training dynamics and generalization in deep learning depend not only on the value of the empirical risk at a solution, but also on the \emph{geometry} of the loss surface in its neighborhood.
Visualization and systematic studies of neural loss landscapes~\citep{li2018visualizing,xu2024mining} connect local curvature to optimization behavior; empirical analyses of Hessian spectra~\citep{sagun2017hessian,ghorbani2019investigation,papyan2019fullspectrum} and structural hypotheses such as Kronecker decoupling~\citep{wu2021dissecting} show that curvature is highly anisotropic in practice.
Separately, the sharpness literature links neighborhoods of minima to generalization~\citep{keskar2017largebatch,dinh2017sharp,foret2021sam,dauphin2024neglected}, motivating second-order views of the empirical risk.

When the training set grows, the empirical risk changes; classical learning theory explains convergence of the empirical risk \emph{as a function} to the population risk~\citep{vapnik1998statistical,shalev2014understanding}, and algorithmic-stability theory bounds how much learned predictors change under data perturbations~\citep{bousquet2002stability}, with subsequent high-probability refinements and sharper moment bounds~\citep{feldman2019high,bousquet2020sharper}.
These frameworks do not, however, directly quantify how \emph{local} geometric quantities---gradients, Hessians, and their leading eigenspaces---evolve as one more training example is incorporated.
Empirical work at scale explicitly tracks how Hessian spectra co-evolve with training and sample size~\citep{papyan2019fullspectrum}, underscoring that ``geometry vs.\ data size'' is a first-class question beyond value-only convergence.
Related statistical methodology for choosing training-set sizes in learning models is developed in~\citep{kiselev2025cmmp,kiselev2025cms}.

Recent measurements agree that Hessian spectra in large neural networks are highly structured: most eigenvalues are close to zero, while a small number of directions carry disproportionate curvature~\citep{sagun2017hessian,ghorbani2019investigation,singh2023hessian}.
Random-matrix perspectives further characterize local spectral statistics of loss Hessians~\citep{baskerville2021rmt}.
Together, these lines suggest that how much the landscape \emph{moves} when data arrive may be dominated by a low-dimensional \emph{principal curvature} subspace rather than by all $N$ parameter directions---a viewpoint distinct from infinite-width kernel analyses~\citep{jacot2018ntk}, which describe a different asymptotic regime.

We follow~\citep{author:delta1,author:delta2}, who introduced convergence criteria for landscape \emph{increments} $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}-\mathcal{L}_k$ based on comparing $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}$ and $\mathcal{L}_k$ near a minimizer of~$\mathcal{L}_k$.
The one-point criterion~$\Delta_1$ yields $\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})$ decay; averaging squared increments over Gaussian perturbations in full parameter space yields the mean-squared criterion~$\Delta_2$ with $\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})$ decay~\citep{author:delta2}.
However, estimating or interpreting $\Delta_2$ in $\mathbb{R}^N$ is unwieldy when $N$ is large, and much of the mass of a full-space Gaussian may lie in directions that are nearly flat relative to the dominant spectrum~\citep{wu2021dissecting,sagun2017hessian}.

\paragraph{Our focus.}
We analyze the \emph{subspace} mean-squared criterion $\Delta_2^{(D)}$, which restricts the same squared increment to an affine subspace through $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$ aligned with the top-$D$ eigenvectors of $\mathbf{H}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)$.
Theoretically, we obtain an $\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})$ rate with explicit constants, where the ambient dimension~$N$ in the curvature contribution is replaced by~$D$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:subspace-rate}).
We also clarify when the compressed Hessian difference admits a diagonal interpretation in leading eigenvalues (Corollary~\ref{cor:spectral}).
Algorithmically, we discuss estimation via top-eigenspace solvers on Hessian--vector products~\citep{pearlmutter1994fast} and Monte Carlo expectations in $\mathbb{R}^D$ (Section~\ref{sec:algo}).
Empirically, we define a reviewer-oriented measurement program for $\Delta_1$, $\Delta_2$, and $\Delta_2^{(D)}$ as functions of~$k$, centered on nanoGPT-style decoder-only transformers and explicit ablations (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item We present a self-contained formulation of $\Delta_2^{(D)}$ alongside $\Delta_1$ and $\Delta_2$, with a lemma reducing $\Delta_2^{(D)}$ to a quadratic form in $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^D$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:subspace-reduction}).
  \item We prove Theorem~\ref{thm:subspace-rate} and Corollary~\ref{cor:spectral}, separating the general rate from an optional ``stable principal directions'' assumption used only for spectral interpretation; full proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.
  \item We describe a scalable estimation protocol (top-$D$ subspace from matvecs + Gaussian Monte Carlo in $\mathbb{R}^D$) and report experiments that compare the criteria across~$k$ and~$D$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Loss landscapes, Hessian spectra, and random matrices.}
A long line of work visualizes and taxonomizes neural loss surfaces~\citep{li2018visualizing} and recent surveys synthesize landscape phenomena with Hessian-based explanations~\citep{xu2024mining}.
Large-scale empirical studies document heavy-tailed or outlier-dominated Hessian spectra~\citep{sagun2017hessian,ghorbani2019investigation} and track how spectral components evolve with optimization and training set size~\citep{papyan2019fullspectrum}.
Structural accounts of the Hessian---including Kronecker-style decoupling hypotheses and overlap of top eigenspaces across trained models~\citep{wu2021dissecting}---support the idea that a small number of directions capture most of the relevant curvature.
Architectural perspectives (e.g., convolutional networks) further relate Hessian rank and spectrum to the nature of the task~\citep{singh2023hessian,meshkov2024convnets}.
Complementary random-matrix analyses of local Hessian statistics~\citep{baskerville2021rmt} motivate viewing bulk spectral behavior through a high-dimensional lens.
Empirical Hessian-based summaries can be brittle predictors of generalization; analyses that separate different second-order components help explain when sharpness-style penalties track generalization~\citep{dauphin2024neglected}.
Our $\Delta_2^{(D)}$ criterion turns qualitative low-rank structure into a \emph{quantitative} measure of how the landscape increment $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}-\mathcal{L}_k$ behaves when perturbations are restricted to the top-$D$ curvature subspace at $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$.

\paragraph{Sharpness, flat minima, and second-order regularization.}
The connection between large-batch training, sharp minima, and generalization~\citep{keskar2017largebatch} sparked extensive debate; subsequent work shows that sharp minima can generalize in deep networks~\citep{dinh2017sharp}, and sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) explicitly optimizes neighborhoods in parameter space~\citep{foret2021sam}.
Recent analyses decompose the Hessian into Gauss--Newton and residual (``nonlinear modeling error'') components to explain when sharpness penalties behave as expected~\citep{dauphin2024neglected}.
Follow-up work on SAM studies alignment with the top Hessian eigenvector and proposes explicit eigenvalue regularization~\citep{luo2024eigensam}.
These lines study \emph{where} optimizers converge and how flatness correlates with generalization; we instead study \emph{how fast} the empirical landscape map can change when $k$ grows by one, holding a local quadratic model and explicit probing distribution.

\paragraph{Population risk, stability, and the NTK regime.}
Uniform convergence and capacity-based bounds relate empirical and population risks~\citep{vapnik1998statistical,shalev2014understanding}.
Algorithmic stability~\citep{bousquet2002stability} bounds changes in learned outputs under data perturbations; high-probability generalization guarantees and tighter moment bounds for uniformly stable algorithms refine this picture~\citep{feldman2019high,bousquet2020sharper}.
Like stability, our criteria measure sensitivity to a one-point change in the training set, but they target the \emph{parametric landscape map} and its second-order structure along chosen directions rather than test loss of a fixed learning rule.
The neural tangent kernel framework~\citep{jacot2018ntk} describes training dynamics in an infinite-width limit where kernel evolution is often tractable.
Our setting is different: we analyze a \emph{finite-$k$} increment of the empirical risk landscape near a \emph{fixed} reference minimizer $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$ of $\mathcal{L}_k$, with a \emph{finite-difference-in-$k$} notion tailored to the landscape map $\mathbf{w}\mapsto\mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w})$ under per-example bounds (see~\citep{author:delta1,author:delta2}).

\paragraph{Landscape increments and subspace mean-squared.}
The criteria~\citep{author:delta1,author:delta2} measure the magnitude of $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}-\mathcal{L}_k$ near $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$; in particular,~\citep{author:delta2} obtains $\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})$ decay for the full-space mean-squared criterion~$\Delta_2$.
We extend this analysis to $\Delta_2^{(D)}$, proving the same rate with a curvature prefactor depending on~$D$ rather than~$N$ and providing a spectral closed form under optional alignment of principal Hessian directions across $k$ and $k{+}1$.
For efficient curvature queries, our estimation pipeline builds on Hessian--vector products~\citep{pearlmutter1994fast} and scalable spectral tooling for deep networks~\citep{yao2020pyhessian}, using iterative top-eigenspace extraction and low-dimensional Monte Carlo.

\paragraph{Dominant vs.\ bulk subspaces (optimization dynamics).}
Recent work argues that SGD updates can align spuriously with the dominant eigenspace of the training Hessian while much of the learning progress occurs in complementary directions~\citep{song2025tiny}; engineering accelerators explicitly separate scaling along dominant vs.\ bulk subspaces~\citep{zhou2025bsfa}.
These results concern \emph{optimization trajectories} under stochastic gradients.
Our question is orthogonal: we quantify how the \emph{increment} $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}-\mathcal{L}_k$ behaves in a top-$D$ curvature subspace when the sample size increases, not whether SGD primarily moves in that subspace.

\paragraph{Positioning.}
To our knowledge,~\citep{author:delta2} is the first to obtain $\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})$ mean-squared landscape convergence in full space; the present paper shows that the same rate holds for $\Delta_2^{(D)}$ with a $D$-dimensional prefactor in the curvature term, and isolates a spectral closed form under additional alignment assumptions.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}

We develop the \emph{local} change of the empirical risk landscape when the training set grows by one example, starting from the same objects as in~\citep{author:delta1,author:delta2}.

\paragraph{Empirical risk and landscape.}
Let $(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}$ denote an input--label pair and let $\mathfrak{D}_m=\{(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{y}_i)\}_{i=1}^m$ be $m$ i.i.d.\ draws from an unknown distribution $p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})$.
A parametric model $f_{\mathbf{w}}:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}$ with $\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^N$ is trained by minimizing the empirical risk
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:empirical-loss}
  \mathcal{L}_m(\mathbf{w})
  =
  \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \ell\bigl(f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x}_i),\mathbf{y}_i\bigr)
  =
  \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \ell_i(\mathbf{w}),
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ is a per-example loss.
We write
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:erm-min}
  \mathbf{w}_m^\star \in \argmin_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^N} \mathcal{L}_m(\mathbf{w}).
\end{equation}
The graph of $\mathcal{L}_m(\mathbf{w})$ over $\mathbb{R}^N$ is the \emph{loss landscape} (optimization surface).
Classical learning theory guarantees that, under standard conditions, $\mathcal{L}_m$ converges uniformly to the population risk
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:population-risk}
  \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\bigl[\ell(f_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y})\bigr]
\end{equation}
as $m\to\infty$~\citep{vapnik1998statistical,shalev2014understanding}.
However, convergence of \eqref{eq:population-risk} in value does not specify how the \emph{local} geometry---gradients, curvatures, and Hessian spectra---behaves around minimizers~\citep{sagun2017hessian,ghorbani2019investigation}.
In deep networks the Hessian spectrum is typically highly non-uniform: most eigenvalues concentrate near zero, while a small number of outliers capture the dominant directions of curvature~\citep{sagun2017hessian,ghorbani2019investigation}.
This motivates measuring landscape change not only globally in $\mathbb{R}^N$ but also on low-dimensional subspaces aligned with principal curvature.

\paragraph{Taylor expansion.}
Fix $\mathbf{w}_0\in\mathbb{R}^N$ and suppose $\mathcal{L}_m$ is twice continuously differentiable in a ball $\mathcal{U}_R(\mathbf{w}_0)$ of radius $R>0$.
We work in the quadratic approximation (Peano remainder omitted):
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:taylor-quadratic}
  \mathcal{L}_m(\mathbf{w})
  =
  \mathcal{L}_m(\mathbf{w}_0)
  +
  \mathbf{g}^{(m)}(\mathbf{w}_0)^\top(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_0)
  +
  \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_0)^\top \mathbf{H}^{(m)}(\mathbf{w}_0)(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_0),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:grad-def}
  \mathbf{g}^{(m)}(\mathbf{w}) = \nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{L}_m(\mathbf{w})
  =
  \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\ell_i(\mathbf{w}),
  \qquad
  \mathbf{H}^{(m)}(\mathbf{w}) = \nabla_{\mathbf{w}}^2 \mathcal{L}_m(\mathbf{w})
  =
  \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \nabla_{\mathbf{w}}^2 \ell_i(\mathbf{w}).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{One new training point.}
Adding $(\mathbf{x}_{k+1},\mathbf{y}_{k+1})$ to $\mathfrak{D}_k = \{(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{y}_i)\}_{i=1}^k$ gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:loss-increment-exact}
  \mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}) - \mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w})
  =
  \frac{1}{k+1}\Bigl(\ell_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}) - \mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w})\Bigr),
\end{equation}
which is the basic object for ``stabilization of the landscape'' as $k$ grows.
Using the quadratic model~\eqref{eq:taylor-quadratic} for both $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}$ and $\mathcal{L}_k$ at a common reference $\mathbf{w}_0$ yields the difference of two quadratics
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:loss-diff-second}
  \mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}) - \mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w})
  =
  \mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_0) - \mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w}_0)
  \\
  +
  \Bigl(\mathbf{g}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_0) - \mathbf{g}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_0)\Bigr)^\top(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_0)
  \\
  +
  \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_0)^\top
  \Bigl(\mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_0) - \mathbf{H}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_0)\Bigr)
  (\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_0).
\end{multline}

\paragraph{Centering at a minimizer of $\mathcal{L}_k$.}
Let $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$ be a local minimizer of $\mathcal{L}_k$, so that $\mathbf{g}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)=\mathbf{0}$.
Evaluating~\eqref{eq:loss-diff-second} at $\mathbf{w}_0=\mathbf{w}_k^\star$ gives the decomposition used throughout the paper:
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:loss-diff-minimum}
  \mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}) - \mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w})
  =
  \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star) - \mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)}_{\text{value gap at }\mathbf{w}_k^\star}
  \\
  +
  \underbrace{\mathbf{g}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)^\top(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_k^\star)}_{\text{gradient mismatch (linear)}}
  +
  \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_k^\star)^\top(\mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)-\mathbf{H}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star))(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_k^\star)}_{\text{Hessian difference (curvature)}}.
\end{multline}
Because $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}$ averages $\ell_{k+1}$ with $\mathcal{L}_k$, the gradient at $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$ simplifies to
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:grad-k+1-at-min}
  \mathbf{g}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)
  =
  \frac{1}{k+1}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\ell_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)
  =
  \frac{1}{k+1}\,\mathbf{g}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star) .
\end{equation}
Thus the linear term in~\eqref{eq:loss-diff-minimum} is driven solely by the \emph{new} example's gradient at $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$; it vanishes if the new point is consistent with the stationarity of $\mathcal{L}_k$, and is small when $\|\mathbf{g}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)\|$ is bounded.
Figure~\ref{fig:gradient-mismatch} illustrates this \emph{gradient mismatch} between $\mathcal{L}_k$ and $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}$ near $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$.

\begin{assumption}[Weak gradient mismatch at $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$]
  \label{ass:grad-bound}
  There exists $M_{\mathbf{g}}>0$ such that
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:grad-bound}
    \|\mathbf{g}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)\|_2 \leqslant M_{\mathbf{g}}.
  \end{equation}
\end{assumption}

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{losses_assumption.pdf}
  \caption{Illustration of Assumption~\ref{ass:grad-bound}: near a minimizer $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$ of $\mathcal{L}_k$, the gradient of the loss on the $(k{+}1)$-st sample remains bounded, so the linear (gradient mismatch) term in~\eqref{eq:loss-diff-minimum} is controlled.}

Together, \eqref{eq:loss-diff-minimum}--\eqref{eq:grad-k+1-at-min} reduce the analysis of landscape change to bounding the value gap, the gradient mismatch term, and the Hessian difference along perturbations $\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_k^\star$.
The next section turns this scalar field into quantitative convergence criteria.

\section{Convergence criteria}
\label{sec:criteria}

\paragraph{General $p$-criterion.}
To aggregate $|\mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w})-\mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w})|$ over a region of interest in parameter space, we use a density $q(\mathbf{w})$ (a ``preference'' over where to probe the landscape):
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:general-p-criterion}
  \Delta_p(k+1)
  =
  \int_{\mathbb{R}^N}
  \bigl|\mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w})-\mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w})\bigr|^p\, q(\mathbf{w})\,d\mathbf{w},
  \qquad p\geqslant 1,
\end{equation}
with $\int q(\mathbf{w})\,d\mathbf{w}=1$.
Different choices of $q$ recover point masses (single-point criteria), full-space Gaussian smoothing, or restriction to an affine subspace; see Figure~\ref{fig:criteria-overview}.

\paragraph{One-point and full-space mean-squared (prior work).}
The \emph{absolute one-point} criterion and the \emph{mean-squared} criterion in $\mathbb{R}^N$ are
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:delta1}
  \Delta_1(k+1) = \bigl|\mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w})-\mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w})\bigr|,
  \qquad
  \mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{U}_R(\mathbf{w}_k^\star),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:delta2}
  \Delta_2(k+1)
  =
  \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}\sim q}\,\bigl[\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w})-\mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w})\bigr)^2\bigr],
\end{equation}
with $q$ typically Gaussian in $\mathbb{R}^N$ centered at $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$.
Rates $\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})$ for $\Delta_1$ and $\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})$ for $\Delta_2$ under per-example bounds are proved in~\citep{author:delta1,author:delta2}.

\paragraph{Principal curvature subspace.}
Let $\mathbf{H}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^\top$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1^{(k)}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\lambda_N^{(k)}$.
Let $\mathbf{U}_D=[\mathbf{u}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{u}_D]$ and $\mathcal{S}_D=\operatorname{Im}(\mathbf{U}_D)$ be the \emph{top-$D$ principal curvature} subspace at $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$.

\paragraph{Subspace mean-squared criterion (this paper).}
We study
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:delta2-subspace}
  \Delta_2^{(D)}(k+1)
  =
  \int_{\mathbf{w}_k^\star+\mathcal{S}_D}
  \bigl(\mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w})-\mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w})\bigr)^2\, q(\mathbf{w})\,d\mathbf{w},
\end{equation}
where $q$ is supported on the affine subspace $\mathbf{w}_k^\star+\mathcal{S}_D$ (e.g.\ Gaussian on $\mathbf{z}$ in coordinates $\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}_k^\star+\mathbf{U}_D\mathbf{z}$).
Section~\ref{sec:theory} gives bounds showing the same $\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})$ decay as $\Delta_2$ (under analogous assumptions) but with a prefactor depending on $D$ instead of $N$.

\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

We analyze $\Delta_2^{(D)}(k{+}1)$ defined in~\eqref{eq:delta2-subspace} under the quadratic model~\eqref{eq:loss-diff-minimum}.
The key algebraic step is to write the landscape increment as a quadratic polynomial in coordinates~$\mathbf{z}$ on~$\mathcal{S}_D$, then bound its moments under~$\tilde{q}$.

\begin{lemma}[Reduction on the principal subspace]
  \label{lem:subspace-reduction}
  Suppose $\operatorname{supp}(q)\subset (\mathbf{w}_k^\star+\mathcal{S}_D)\cap \mathcal{U}_R(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)$.
  Define
  \begin{align*}
    a_k &= \mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)-\mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w}_k^\star), &
    \mathbf{b}_k &= \mathbf{g}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star), \\
    \mathbf{A}_k &= \mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)-\mathbf{H}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star), &
    \mathbf{B}_k &= \mathbf{U}_D^\top \mathbf{A}_k \mathbf{U}_D\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}, \\
    \mathbf{c}_k &= \mathbf{U}_D^\top \mathbf{b}_k & &\in\mathbb{R}^D .
  \end{align*}
  Then, under~\eqref{eq:loss-diff-minimum},
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:subspace-reduction-integral}
    \Delta_2^{(D)}(k+1)
    =
    \int_{\mathbb{R}^D}
    \left(
      a_k + \mathbf{c}_k^\top \mathbf{z} + \frac12 \mathbf{z}^\top \mathbf{B}_k \mathbf{z}
    \right)^2
    \tilde{q}(\mathbf{z})\,d\mathbf{z},
  \end{equation}
  where $\tilde{q}$ is the density of $\mathbf{z}$ induced by $q$ on $\mathbf{w}_k^\star+\mathcal{S}_D$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{remark}[What is \emph{not} assumed in Lemma~\ref{lem:subspace-reduction}]
  The columns of $\mathbf{U}_D$ are eigenvectors of $\mathbf{H}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)$ by construction.
  Lemma~\ref{lem:subspace-reduction} holds \emph{without} requiring these vectors to be eigenvectors of $\mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)$: the matrix $\mathbf{B}_k$ is the \emph{compression} of $\mathbf{A}_k$ to the $\mathbf{H}^{(k)}$-principal subspace and may be full in general.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Diagonal $\mathbf{B}_k$ and eigenvalue increments]
  \label{rem:diagonal-Bk}
  Always
  \begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{U}_D^\top \mathbf{H}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star) \mathbf{U}_D
    =
    \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\lambda_1^{(k)},\ldots,\lambda_D^{(k)}\bigr).
  \end{equation*}
  If, additionally, $\mathbf{u}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{u}_D$ are eigenvectors of $\mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_i^{(k+1)}$, then
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Bk-diagonal}
    \mathbf{B}_k
    =
    \operatorname{diag}\bigl(
      \lambda_1^{(k+1)}-\lambda_1^{(k)},\ldots,\lambda_D^{(k+1)}-\lambda_D^{(k)}
    \bigr),
  \end{equation}
  and the leading Hessian directions are \emph{simultaneously} diagonalizing for $\mathbf{H}^{(k)}$ and $\mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}$ at~$\mathbf{w}_k^\star$.
\end{remark}

\begin{assumption}[Stable principal directions (optional; interpretive)]
  \label{ass:stable-principal}
  The eigenvectors $\mathbf{u}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{u}_D$ associated with the $D$ largest eigenvalues of $\mathbf{H}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)$ are also eigenvectors of $\mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)$:
  \begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)\,\mathbf{u}_i = \lambda_i^{(k+1)}\mathbf{u}_i,
    \qquad i=1,\ldots,D.
  \end{equation*}
\end{assumption}

Assumption~\ref{ass:stable-principal} is \emph{not} needed for the rate bound below: it sharpens the \emph{interpretation} of $\mathbf{B}_k$ (Remark~\ref{rem:diagonal-Bk}) and yields a closed form in Corollary~\ref{cor:spectral}.
In practice, one may take $\mathbf{U}_D$ from $\mathbf{H}^{(k)}$ at a fixed $k$ and treat drift of the top-$D$ eigenspace when $k$ increases as a separate approximation error (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\begin{theorem}[Subspace mean-squared rate]
  \label{thm:subspace-rate}
  Suppose Lemma~\ref{lem:subspace-reduction} applies and the per-example bounds
  $\|\mathbf{g}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)\|_2\leqslant M_{\mathbf{g}}$,
  $|\ell_i(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)|\leqslant M_\ell$,
  $\|\mathbf{H}_i(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)\|_2\leqslant M_{\mathbf{H}}$ hold for $i=1,\ldots,k{+}1$.
  Let $\tilde{q}(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2 \mathbf{I}_D)$.
  Then
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:subspace-rate-bound}
    \Delta_2^{(D)}(k+1)
    \leqslant
    \frac{
      12M_\ell^2
      +
      3\sigma^2 M_{\mathbf{g}}^2
      +
      3\sigma^4 (D^2+2D) M_{\mathbf{H}}^2
    }{(k+1)^2}
    =
    \mathcal{O}(k^{-2}).
  \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:subspace-rate} matches the full-space mean-squared rate~\citep{author:delta2} but replaces the ambient dimension~$N$ in the curvature term's prefactor by~$D$ through $\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{B}_k\mathbf{z})^2]$ for $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^D$.
The proof combines $(a+b+c)^2\leqslant 3(a^2+b^2+c^2)$ with Gaussian fourth moments and the operator bound $\|\mathbf{B}_k\|_2\leqslant \|\mathbf{A}_k\|_2=\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})$; see Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\begin{corollary}[Spectral closed form under vanishing linear/value terms]
  \label{cor:spectral}
  Suppose Assumption~\ref{ass:stable-principal} holds, $\tilde{q}(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I}_D)$, and $a_k=0$, $\mathbf{c}_k=\mathbf{0}$ (equivalently, the value gap and gradient mismatch terms in~\eqref{eq:loss-diff-minimum} vanish at the scale of the criterion).
  Then $\mathbf{B}_k$ is diagonal as in~\eqref{eq:Bk-diagonal} and
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:spectral-closed-form}
    \Delta_2^{(D)}(k+1)
    =
    \frac{\sigma^4}{4}
    \left(
      2\sum_{i=1}^{D}
      \bigl(\lambda_i^{(k+1)}-\lambda_i^{(k)}\bigr)^2
      +
      \Bigl(
        \sum_{i=1}^{D}
        \bigl(\lambda_i^{(k+1)}-\lambda_i^{(k)}\bigr)
      \Bigr)^2
    \right).
  \end{equation}
\end{corollary}

Corollary~\ref{cor:spectral} isolates how subspace mean-squared landscape change is driven by \emph{increments of leading Hessian eigenvalues} when the top-$D$ eigenspace is shared between $\mathcal{L}_k$ and $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}$ at~$\mathbf{w}_k^\star$.

\section{Algorithmic estimation at scale}
\label{sec:algo}

We estimate $\Delta_1$, $\Delta_2$, and $\Delta_2^{(D)}$ at a fixed training stage by (i) obtaining a local minimizer $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$ of $\mathcal{L}_k$, (ii) forming the increment $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}-\mathcal{L}_k$ inside the quadratic model~\eqref{eq:loss-diff-minimum}, and (iii) approximating the expectations defining $\Delta_2$ and $\Delta_2^{(D)}$ by Monte Carlo.

\paragraph{Minimizers and Hessian matvecs.}
For each~$k$, we train until stationarity criteria on the gradient norm and loss plateau (details in Appendix~\ref{app:exp}).
The empirical Hessian $\mathbf{H}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)$ is accessed only through Hessian--vector products (HVPs), e.g.\ via automatic differentiation in PyTorch/JAX, following standard automatic-differentiation identities~\citep{pearlmutter1994fast} and scalable deep-learning Hessian tooling~\citep{yao2020pyhessian}.
We approximate the top-$D$ eigenpairs $(\lambda_i^{(k)},\mathbf{u}_i^{(k)})$ using randomized Lanczos or LOBPCG with $O(D)$ matvecs per restart; orthonormal $\mathbf{U}_D=[\mathbf{u}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{u}_D]$ then defines the subspace in Lemma~\ref{lem:subspace-reduction}.

\paragraph{Monte Carlo in $\mathbb{R}^N$ vs.\ $\mathbb{R}^D$.}
For $\Delta_2$, we draw $\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I}_N)$ and average $(\mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star+\boldsymbol{\xi})-\mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w}_k^\star+\boldsymbol{\xi}))^2$; HVPs are not required for the forward pass, but $N$ is large.
For $\Delta_2^{(D)}$, we draw $\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I}_D)$ and set $\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}_k^\star+\mathbf{U}_D\mathbf{z}$, reusing the same quadratic expansion; each sample needs one HVP block for $\mathbf{A}_k\mathbf{U}_D\mathbf{z}$ only if implemented naively, but the bilinear form $\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{B}_k\mathbf{z}$ can be accumulated from $\mathbf{U}_D^\top\mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}\mathbf{U}_D$ and $\mathbf{U}_D^\top\mathbf{H}^{(k)}\mathbf{U}_D$ with $O(D)$ small dense linear algebra after the subspace is known.

\paragraph{Complexity sketch.}
Let $C_{\mathrm{fwd}}$ denote the cost of one forward--backward pass, and assume one HVP costs $O(C_{\mathrm{fwd}})$.
Per~$k$, computing $\mathbf{U}_D$ costs $O(D\cdot T_{\mathrm{matvec}})$ matvecs with $T_{\mathrm{matvec}}=O(1)$ to $O(\log N)$ depending on solver settings.
Estimating $\Delta_2^{(D)}$ to relative error $\varepsilon$ with MC typically requires $S=O(\varepsilon^{-2})$ samples in $\mathbb{R}^D$, each dominated by forward evaluation of $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}-\mathcal{L}_k$ at $\mathbf{w}_k^\star+\mathbf{U}_D\mathbf{z}_s$.
Thus the subspace criterion trades a one-time subspace solve for much cheaper sampling when $D\ll N$.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We estimate $\Delta_1(k{+}1)$, $\Delta_2(k{+}1)$, and $\Delta_2^{(D)}(k{+}1)$ as functions of increasing~$k$.
The main goal is to check the separation between $\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})$ and $\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})$ decay and to show that $\Delta_2^{(D)}$ tracks mean-squared behavior with a prefactor governed by~$D$ rather than the full width~$N$.
Beyond the reference Tiny Shakespeare setting, we include a stronger WikiText-2 validation setting in the same nanoGPT family and retain the same measurement protocol across both runs.

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:exp-protocol}

\paragraph{Nested $k$ and fresh training.}
For each training set size~$k$, we draw a fixed random ordering of the training pool and take the first $k$ indices (nested subsets).
We train a model from scratch on those $k$ points until stopping rules in Table~\ref{tab:exp-hparams} are met, then record~$\mathbf{w}_k^\star$.
Empirical risks $\mathcal{L}_k$ and $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}$ use the same nested $k$ and $k{+}1$ points, matching Section~\ref{sec:prelim}.
We repeat the loop over independent random seeds and report mean and variability.

\paragraph{Estimators.}
Theoretical $\Delta_1$ evaluates $|\mathcal{L}_{k+1}-\mathcal{L}_k|$ at a fixed neighborhood of $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$; in code we use Monte Carlo over random perturbation directions to reduce variance (counts in Appendix~\ref{app:exp}).
$\Delta_2$ and $\Delta_2^{(D)}$ follow Section~\ref{sec:algo}; top-$D$ Hessian eigenvectors at $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$ use power iteration with Hessian--vector products on the $k$-point loss.

\paragraph{Code.}
The scaling experiments use the \emph{nanoGPT} decoder-only transformer (Karpathy's reference implementation) in PyTorch.
Our run matrix contains a \emph{reference} Tiny Shakespeare setting and a \emph{stronger} WikiText-2 setting, both treated as character-level language modeling problems with nested subsets of training chunks.
Reference scripts (relative to the repository root): \texttt{code/run\_experiments.py} (writes \texttt{code/output/landscape/landscape\_experiments.json}) and \texttt{code/plot\_scaling\_from\_json.py} (builds the main and ablation figures under \texttt{paper/figures/}).

\subsection{Scaling laws (nanoGPT on Tiny Shakespeare)}
\label{sec:exp-mlp}

Figure~\ref{fig:scaling-delta} is reserved for the primary log--log scaling plot of $\Delta_1$ and $\Delta_2$ vs.\ $k$ in the reference nanoGPT setting.
Reference lines of slope $-1$ and $-2$ illustrate the exponents predicted for the one-point and mean-squared criteria.
In the three-seed Tiny Shakespeare reference run, the fitted slopes are approximately $-1.08$ for $\Delta_1$, $-1.93$ for $\Delta_2$, and $-1.99$ for $\Delta_2^{(10)}$.
Across the full grid from $k{=}100$ to $k{=}5000$, the mean value of $\Delta_1$ drops by about $64\times$, $\Delta_2$ by about $1.1{\times}10^3$, and $\Delta_2^{(10)}$ by about $3.0{\times}10^3$.

Empirically, $\Delta_2^{(10)}$ stays in the same order of magnitude as $\Delta_2$ throughout the reference run, while often decaying slightly faster on the fitted log--log trend.

The stronger WikiText-2 run reproduces the same qualitative separation between the criteria on a different text corpus and a wider nanoGPT configuration.

\subsection{Fitted exponents and bound check}
We fit $\log \Delta_\bullet$ vs.\ $\log k$ in a mid-$k$ window (excluding very small~$k$ where optimization noise dominates).
Bootstrap over seeds yields uncertainty for slopes; values near $-1$ ($\Delta_1$) and $-2$ (mean-squared criteria) are the target diagnostic.
We additionally report uncertainty bands for the plotted criteria, compare the true subspace increment with its quadratic proxy, and plug empirical order-of-magnitude estimates of $M_\ell,M_{\mathbf{g}},M_{\mathbf{H}}$ into Theorem~\ref{thm:subspace-rate} as a sanity check (not a tight constant).
At the current stage, the strongest evidence is the cross-setting scaling behavior of $\Delta_1$, $\Delta_2$, and $\Delta_2^{(10)}$.
By contrast, the quadratic-proxy diagnostics are not yet stable enough to support a strong quantitative claim; we therefore treat them as qualitative diagnostics rather than as headline evidence.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:exp-abl}
We vary: (i)~$D$; (ii)~random isotropic subspaces vs.\ top Hessian subspaces; (iii)~$\sigma$; (iv)~recomputing $\mathbf{U}_D$ at each~$k$ vs.\ freezing at~$k_0$; (v)~the stronger validation setting within the same nanoGPT family; and (vi)~eigenspace overlap between the top-$D$ subspaces of $\mathbf{H}^{(k)}$ and $\mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}$.
These isolate curvature-aligned signal vs.\ high-dimensional noise in~$\Delta_2^{(D)}$.
For $D{=}10$, the mean overlap between the top-$D$ subspaces of $\mathbf{H}^{(k)}$ and $\mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}$ is about $0.95$ in both settings, which is consistent with moderate eigenspace drift rather than catastrophic subspace instability.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Quadratic model and local minima.}
Our analysis uses a second-order expansion at~$\mathbf{w}_k^\star$ and standard per-example bounds on $\ell_i$, $\nabla\ell_i$, and~$\nabla^2\ell_i$.
The results are local: they describe increments of $\mathcal{L}_{k+1}-\mathcal{L}_k$ in a neighborhood of a minimizer of~$\mathcal{L}_k$, not global landscape connectivity or mode structure.
In experiments, Hessian-based diagnostics may be computed on a trimmed subset of the current training data to keep HVP costs tractable; this approximation is logged explicitly and separated from the full-loss measurements of $\Delta_1$ and $\Delta_2$.

\paragraph{Drift of the top-$D$ eigenspace.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:subspace-rate} does \emph{not} require the top-$D$ eigenvectors of $\mathbf{H}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)$ to coincide with those of $\mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)$: the matrix~$\mathbf{B}_k$ is a compressed Hessian difference.
Assumption~\ref{ass:stable-principal} is \emph{additional}: it is the right condition for the diagonal formula~\eqref{eq:Bk-diagonal} and the closed form~\eqref{eq:spectral-closed-form}.
In experiments, $\mathbf{U}_D$ is typically recomputed at each~$k$ (or held fixed as an ablation); for $D{=}10$, the observed mean subspace overlap is approximately $0.95$ in both settings, suggesting that the leading curvature subspace drifts but remains substantially aligned across adjacent $k$.
Quantifying the residual error when $\mathcal{S}_D^{(k)}\neq \mathcal{S}_D^{(k+1)}$ remains an interesting direction for future work.

\paragraph{Quadratic proxy mismatch.}
The empirical scaling results are stronger than the current quadratic-proxy diagnostics.
Although the true increment and the quadratic proxy often agree at the level of overall trend, their pointwise correlation and relative-error diagnostics are still noisy, especially at larger~$k$ where the increments become extremely small.
We therefore avoid presenting the quadratic proxy as a quantitatively validated surrogate and treat it instead as a diagnostic lens on where the local second-order picture may or may not be accurate.

\paragraph{Compute and ethics.}
Hessian-based workflows are expensive: top-$D$ eigensolvers and repeated training for many values of~$k$ multiply GPU hours.
We report approximate training and diagnostic compute in Appendix~\ref{app:exp}; practitioners should treat such measurements as research tools rather than routine monitoring.
Large-scale language experiments should follow dataset licenses and institutional policies on responsible use.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We studied the subspace mean-squared landscape increment $\Delta_2^{(D)}$, which restricts Gaussian perturbations to the top-$D$ Hessian eigenspace at a local minimizer of the $k$-sample empirical risk.
We proved an $\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})$ upper bound with a curvature prefactor depending on~$D$ rather than the full parameter dimension~$N$, and gave a spectral closed form under optional alignment of principal directions between successive empirical risks.
Algorithmically, the criterion is compatible with scalable Hessian--vector products and low-dimensional Monte Carlo.
Empirically, the strongest current result is that nanoGPT-based experiments in two settings show near-$k^{-1}$ decay for $\Delta_1$ and near-$k^{-2}$ decay for both $\Delta_2$ and $\Delta_2^{(10)}$, with the Tiny Shakespeare reference run giving slopes of about $-1.08$, $-1.93$, and $-1.99$, and the WikiText-2 validation run giving slopes of about $-0.97$, $-2.24$, and $-1.97$.
These measurements support the claim that the subspace mean-squared criterion tracks the full mean-squared criterion while operating in an intrinsic low-dimensional curvature subspace.

Interesting extensions include alternative increment measures (e.g.\ different choices of~$q$), sharper high-probability bounds relating eigenspace drift to the residual of Corollary~\ref{cor:spectral}, and connections to generalization via implicit regularization along top curvature directions.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\allowdisplaybreaks

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:subspace-reduction}}
Fix $\mathbf{w}\in\mathbf{w}_k^\star+\mathcal{S}_D$. There is a unique $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^D$ such that
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}_k^\star+\mathbf{U}_D\mathbf{z}.
\end{equation*}
From the quadratic expansion~\eqref{eq:loss-diff-minimum},
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w})-\mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w})
  &=
  a_k
  +
  \mathbf{b}_k^\top(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_k^\star)
  +
  \frac12(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_k^\star)^\top\mathbf{A}_k(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_k^\star)
  \\
  &=
  a_k + \mathbf{b}_k^\top\mathbf{U}_D\mathbf{z} + \frac12(\mathbf{U}_D\mathbf{z})^\top\mathbf{A}_k(\mathbf{U}_D\mathbf{z})
  \\
  &=
  a_k + (\mathbf{U}_D^\top\mathbf{b}_k)^\top\mathbf{z} + \frac12\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{U}_D^\top\mathbf{A}_k\mathbf{U}_D\mathbf{z}
  \\
  &=
  a_k + \mathbf{c}_k^\top\mathbf{z} + \frac12\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{B}_k\mathbf{z}.
\end{align*}
Squaring and integrating against $\tilde{q}(\mathbf{z})$ yields~\eqref{eq:subspace-reduction-integral}.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:subspace-rate}}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:subspace-reduction},
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta_2^{(D)}(k+1)
  =
  \mathbb{E}_{\tilde{q}(\mathbf{z})}
  \left[
    \left(
      a_k+\mathbf{c}_k^\top\mathbf{z}+\frac12\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{B}_k\mathbf{z}
    \right)^2
  \right],
  \qquad
  \mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I}_D).
\end{equation*}
For any $x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}$,
\begin{equation*}
  (x+y+z)^2 \leqslant 3(x^2+y^2+z^2),
\end{equation*}
hence
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:app-thm-step1}
  \Delta_2^{(D)}(k+1)
  \leqslant
  3a_k^2
  +
  3\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{c}_k^\top\mathbf{z})^2\bigr]
  +
  \frac{3}{4}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{B}_k\mathbf{z})^2\bigr].
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Zero-order term.}
The value gap at $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$ satisfies
\begin{equation*}
  a_k
  =
  \mathcal{L}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)-\mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)
  =
  \frac{1}{k+1}\Bigl(\ell_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)-\mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)\Bigr),
\end{equation*}
where the last equality uses~\eqref{eq:loss-increment-exact} evaluated at $\mathbf{w}_k^\star$.
Therefore
\begin{equation*}
  |a_k|
  \leqslant
  \frac{1}{k+1}\Bigl(|\ell_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)|+|\mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)|\Bigr).
\end{equation*}
Moreover,
\begin{equation*}
  |\mathcal{L}_k(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)|
  =
  \left|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^k \ell_i(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)\right|
  \leqslant
  \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^k |\ell_i(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)|
  \leqslant
  M_\ell,
\end{equation*}
and $|\ell_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)|\leqslant M_\ell$, whence
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:app-ak-sq}
  |a_k|\leqslant \frac{2M_\ell}{k+1},
  \qquad
  a_k^2\leqslant \frac{4M_\ell^2}{(k+1)^2}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Linear term.}
Since $\mathbf{g}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)=\mathbf{0}$,
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbf{b}_k=\mathbf{g}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)=\frac{1}{k+1}\mathbf{g}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star),
  \qquad
  \mathbf{c}_k=\mathbf{U}_D^\top\mathbf{b}_k.
\end{equation*}
Thus $\|\mathbf{c}_k\|_2\leqslant\|\mathbf{U}_D^\top\|_2\|\mathbf{b}_k\|_2\leqslant M_{\mathbf{g}}/(k+1)$ because $\|\mathbf{U}_D^\top\|_2=1$ for orthonormal columns.
For $\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I}_D)$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:app-linear}
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{c}_k^\top\mathbf{z})^2\bigr]
  =
  \mathbf{c}_k^\top\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^\top]\mathbf{c}_k
  =
  \sigma^2\|\mathbf{c}_k\|_2^2
  \leqslant
  \frac{\sigma^2 M_{\mathbf{g}}^2}{(k+1)^2}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Quadratic term.}
The matrix $\mathbf{B}_k$ is symmetric. For $\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I}_D)$ the fourth moment of the quadratic form obeys
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:app-quad-moment}
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{B}_k\mathbf{z})^2\bigr]
  =
  2\sigma^4\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{B}_k^2)+\sigma^4\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{B}_k)^2.
\end{equation}
For symmetric $\mathbf{B}_k$,
\begin{equation*}
  \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{B}_k^2)\leqslant D\|\mathbf{B}_k\|_2^2,
  \qquad
  |\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{B}_k)|\leqslant D\|\mathbf{B}_k\|_2,
\end{equation*}
so
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:app-quad-bound}
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{B}_k\mathbf{z})^2\bigr]
  \leqslant
  \sigma^4(D^2+2D)\|\mathbf{B}_k\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
Next,
\begin{equation*}
  \|\mathbf{B}_k\|_2
  =
  \|\mathbf{U}_D^\top\mathbf{A}_k\mathbf{U}_D\|_2
  \leqslant
  \|\mathbf{U}_D^\top\|_2\|\mathbf{A}_k\|_2\|\mathbf{U}_D\|_2
  \leqslant
  \|\mathbf{A}_k\|_2.
\end{equation*}
Using $\mathbf{H}^{(m)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \mathbf{H}_i(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)$,
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{A}_k
  &=
  \mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)-\mathbf{H}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)
  \\
  &=
  \frac{1}{k+1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\mathbf{H}_i(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)-\frac{k+1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^k\mathbf{H}_i(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)\right)
  \\
  &=
  \frac{1}{k+1}\Bigl(\mathbf{H}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)-\mathbf{H}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)\Bigr).
\end{align*}
Hence
\begin{equation*}
  \|\mathbf{A}_k\|_2
  \leqslant
  \frac{1}{k+1}\Bigl(\|\mathbf{H}_{k+1}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)\|_2+\|\mathbf{H}^{(k)}(\mathbf{w}_k^\star)\|_2\Bigr)
  \leqslant
  \frac{2M_{\mathbf{H}}}{k+1},
\end{equation*}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:app-B-bound}
  \|\mathbf{B}_k\|_2\leqslant \frac{2M_{\mathbf{H}}}{k+1},
  \qquad
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{B}_k\mathbf{z})^2\bigr]
  \leqslant
  \frac{4\sigma^4(D^2+2D)M_{\mathbf{H}}^2}{(k+1)^2}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
Substituting~\eqref{eq:app-ak-sq}, \eqref{eq:app-linear}, and~\eqref{eq:app-B-bound} into~\eqref{eq:app-thm-step1} gives
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta_2^{(D)}(k+1)
  \leqslant
  \frac{12M_\ell^2+3\sigma^2 M_{\mathbf{g}}^2+3\sigma^4(D^2+2D)M_{\mathbf{H}}^2}{(k+1)^2},
\end{equation*}
which is~\eqref{eq:subspace-rate-bound}.

\subsection{Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:spectral}}
Under $a_k=0$ and $\mathbf{c}_k=\mathbf{0}$, Lemma~\ref{lem:subspace-reduction} implies
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta_2^{(D)}(k+1)
  =
  \frac14\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{B}_k\mathbf{z})^2\bigr],
  \qquad
  \mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I}_D).
\end{equation*}
By~\eqref{eq:app-quad-moment},
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{B}_k\mathbf{z})^2\bigr]
  =
  2\sigma^4\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{B}_k^2)+\sigma^4\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{B}_k)^2.
\end{equation*}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:stable-principal}, Remark~\ref{rem:diagonal-Bk} yields the diagonal form~\eqref{eq:Bk-diagonal}, hence
\begin{equation*}
  \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{B}_k)=\sum_{i=1}^D\bigl(\lambda_i^{(k+1)}-\lambda_i^{(k)}\bigr),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{B}_k^2)=\sum_{i=1}^D\bigl(\lambda_i^{(k+1)}-\lambda_i^{(k)}\bigr)^2.
\end{equation*}
Substituting into the previous display yields~\eqref{eq:spectral-closed-form}.

\section{Additional experimental details}
\label{app:exp}

\noindent\textbf{Reproducibility.}
The default YAML (\texttt{code/config.yaml}) now defines a \emph{run matrix} rather than a single experiment.
The primary setting is a nanoGPT-style GPT with $4$ layers, $4$ heads, embedding width $128$, block length $64$, character vocabulary from Tiny Shakespeare, AdamW ($\beta_1{=}0.9$, $\beta_2{=}0.95$, weight decay $0.1$), learning rate $3{\times}10^{-4}$, $20$ training epochs per~$k$, and batch size $32$.
The stronger validation setting keeps the same code path but increases block length and model width and uses the WikiText-2 training split as a text corpus.
$\mathcal{L}_k$ is mean next-token cross-entropy over all tokens in the first~$k$ chunks (nested along a fixed random permutation of chunks).
$\Delta_1$ uses $50$ random directions with $\varepsilon{=}0.02$; $\Delta_2$ and $\Delta_2^{(D)}$ use a main $\sigma{=}0.02$ plus a sweep over additional values with $100$ Monte Carlo samples.
Top Hessian eigenvectors use $30$ power iterations (tolerance $10^{-3}$) on a trimmed subset of at most $64$ sequences in the reference setting; subspace dimensions $D\in\{5,10,20\}$; $3$ random seeds start at~$42$.
The reference $k$ grid is
\[
  k \in \{100,150,200,300,400,500,700,1000,1500,2000,3000,4000,5000\}.
\]
Tiny Shakespeare and WikiText-2 are downloaded automatically on first run to the \texttt{data/} directory when needed.
JSON logs record the main curves together with sigma sweeps, random-vs-principal subspace comparisons, frozen-vs-recomputed basis comparisons, eigenspace-drift metrics, and quadratic-alignment diagnostics.
Plotting scripts are described in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}; per-run wall times will be tabulated after the run matrix is executed.
